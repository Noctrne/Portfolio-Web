The first workshop I taught, I prepared forty slides. I used maybe twelve. The students were not short of information; they were short of a way to hold it.

Teaching robotics to high-school students across Indonesia made one thing obvious: a person does not fail to understand because they are not smart. They fail because an earlier assumption is still running quietly in the background.

## Find the wrong assumption

A line-following robot that spins in circles is usually not a motor problem. It is a sensor problem, or a threshold problem, or a student who has not yet noticed that the sensor is pointed at the floor. You cannot fix it by explaining the correct answer louder. You have to find the assumption underneath.

The same is true of teams. When someone keeps missing a deadline, the useful question is rarely “why are you late?” It is “what did you think was due?” The answer is often a different project than the one you had in mind.

## Explain it three ways

I learned to keep three explanations ready: one with a diagram, one with a physical object, and one with a story. Not because repetition is good, but because understanding arrives through different doors. The diagram works for the student who thinks in structure. The object works for the one who needs to touch the problem. The story works for everyone else.

This is slower than a single clear explanation. It is also faster than repeating the same explanation four times to a room that has already stopped listening.

> If the explanation did not land, the explanation is part of the bug.

## What teaching gave back

Patience, mostly. Also a lower tolerance for jargon and a higher tolerance for questions that sound obvious. The questions are usually not obvious; they are the place where the model breaks.

I still teach when I can. It keeps my engineering work honest. A system that only its builder can explain is not finished; it is merely working. Teaching is how I check which one I have.
