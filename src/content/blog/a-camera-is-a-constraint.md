For a month I photographed the same north-facing window. Not because it was interesting, but because I wanted to know what would change if I stopped looking for new subjects.

The answer was almost everything. The wall moved from grey to warm and back again. The shadow of the frame shortened, disappeared, and returned on the other side. A plant I had ignored became a clock.

## Framing is subtraction

A camera does not add drama. It removes options. You choose a rectangle, a moment, and a distance, and everything outside that decision stops existing. The photograph gets better when you accept how little it can hold.

I used to fill the frame because empty space felt like a mistake. Now I leave it there on purpose. A blank wall can do the work of a caption: it tells you where to look and how long to stay.

## Light is a schedule

Photography taught me to treat light as something that happens on a timetable rather than something you switch on. The same room is a different room at eight in the morning and four in the afternoon. If you want a particular version of it, you have to show up for that version.

That is also a decent way to think about design. You are not decorating a page; you are deciding what a person sees first, second, and last. Contrast is a kind of attention.

> The frame is not a window. It is an argument about what matters.

## Habits I keep

- Return to the same place until it stops being background.
- Take one frame after you think you are finished.
- Wait for the light instead of fixing it later.
- Print the image; screens flatter everything.

I still make pictures that do not work. The difference is that I now know why: I was trying to include too much, or I arrived at the wrong hour. Both are fixable. Neither requires a better camera.
